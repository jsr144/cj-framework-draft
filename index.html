Criminal Justice AI Assessment Framework

Practical guidance for agency leaders, policymakers, and procurement officials

A comprehensive framework for responsible AI adoption in criminal justice

Criminal justice agencies face urgent questions about AI adoption: Should we use this tool? What safeguards are needed? This framework provides practical, operational guidance for agency leaders, policymakers, and procurement officials to decide if and how to implement an AI tool.

The Unique Context of Criminal Justice AI

While many AI governance frameworks exist, they often speak in generalities that leave criminal justice practitioners uncertain about specific implementation steps. This framework makes a unique contribution by translating broad AI principles into actionable criminal justice requirements:

  • Constitutional and due process considerations tailored specifically to criminal justice applications
  • Concrete procurement and implementation steps that address the practical realities of agency operations
  • Ready-to-use checklists and templates that can be adapted to your jurisdiction's needs

Using This Framework

This framework is designed for multiple audiences within the criminal justice ecosystem:

Primary Audiences

Agency leaders, policymakers, procurement officials, legal counsel, technology directors, and program managers.

Supporting Stakeholders

Oversight bodies, auditors, community advocates, civil liberties organizations, and vendor partners.

Important Note on AI Classification

This framework is designed for use specifically with AI systems opposed to other common forms of technology or software. Use of this framework assumes that the user already has clarity on whether the tool in question should be classified as AI that could substantially influence decisions (as opposed to purely procedural tools like spell-check or spreadsheets), or that the user has the knowledge or external support necessary to make such a determination.

Different Starting Points

Already Identified a Specific AI System?

By default, this framework assumes you have already identified a specific AI system for evaluation. The assessment phases are designed to evaluate that particular system's characteristics, risks, and opportunities before making procurement and implementation decisions.

Conducting Open Procurement?

If you haven't yet selected a specific vendor or system, use this framework differently: First, conduct a high-level assessment to determine whether your agency should pursue this category of tool at all. Assess each finalist proposal using the risk and opportunity frameworks before making your selection.

Exploring Whether AI Might Help?

If you have no specific tools in mind, begin with Phase 1 (readiness assessment) and the sector context guidance to understand how AI might intersect with your current practices. Consider whether problems might be better addressed through policy changes, training, or increased resources.

A Call for Critical Thinking

This framework provides a structured pathway for critical thinking. The questions, tables, and examples are meant to be illustrative guides, not rigid decision trees. We expect users to:

  • Engage thoughtfully with difficult questions about fairness, bias, and constitutional compliance
  • Challenge assumptions about what technology can and should do in justice settings
  • Be prepared to say no if safeguards cannot be adequately implemented
  • Leverage domain expertise, legal obligations, and contextual nuance throughout the process

Top Takeaways

1

Assess organizational readiness first

Before evaluating any specific AI tool, honestly assess whether your agency has the capacity, expertise, and infrastructure for responsible AI adoption.

2

Screen for prohibited uses immediately

Some AI applications pose unacceptable risks to fundamental rights and must never be deployed, regardless of potential benefits.

3

Use multidisciplinary assessment teams

Include legal, operational, technical, and community representatives in evaluating any AI system, with enhanced expertise for substantial-risk systems.

4

Require rigorous, independent validation

Never rely solely on vendor claims. Demand independent evidence of performance, especially for substantial-risk systems.

5

Establish robust procurement protections

Contracts must address performance standards, demographic equity, data rights, auditability, liability, and termination rights before any system is acquired.

6

Calibrate procurement and deployment for risk

Contracts must include specific terms for performance, data rights, auditing, and liability. The type of AI system and its inputs/outputs help determine the relevant risk-level.

7

Mandate user training

Train operators on the AI's limitations and how to prevent automation bias.

8

Ensure meaningful human oversight

AI must support, not replace, human oversight. Train operators on automation bias and guarantee their authority to override system recommendations.

9

Conduct ongoing assessment and evaluation

Monitor AI performance on an on-going basis and formally reassess it at least annually.

10

Engage the community directly

Ensure that community voice is integrated from the outset of the process.

Phase 1

Assess Your Readiness

Adopting AI is not merely a technical choice; it is a policy decision with profound implications for justice, equity, and public trust. Before evaluating a specific AI tool, your agency should first conduct an honest assessment of its own readiness.

Not every jurisdiction needs to rush toward AI adoption. In some cases, the most responsible decision is to first invest in building foundational capacity.

Key Question

If your agency is not ready to use AI tools responsibly, this framework can help you identify the gaps. Use the AI Readiness Assessment Worksheet to evaluate your organization's current capacity in key areas.

Complete the Readiness Assessment

Evaluate your organization's foundational capacity before beginning an assessment of a specific AI tool.

Key Areas to Assess

  • Data Governance: Clear policies for data quality, collection, storage, and sharing
  • Technical Capacity: Staff expertise and IT infrastructure
  • Legal & Policy Framework: Internal policies and legal counsel
  • Organizational Culture: Leadership buy-in and staff training
  • Community Engagement: Established channels and trust with communities

Important

If this assessment reveals significant gaps, address them before proceeding with AI deployment.

Phase 2

Classification

The goal of this phase is to produce a Classification Memo (see Appendix E) that analyzes the AI system and recommends a path forward.

1

Assemble Your Assessment Team

Required for ALL Systems:

  • An operational leader
  • A legal/constitutional expert
  • End-users

ADD for Substantial Risk Systems:

  • A sector specialist
  • Community representatives
  • A technical expert (for complex systems)
2

Screen for Prohibited Uses

Some AI applications pose an unacceptable risk to fundamental rights and are prohibited in the criminal justice context.

If you answer YES to ANY question below, the system is PROHIBITED.

3

Assess System Complexity and Interpretability

Evaluate the system's technical characteristics. More complex, opaque ("black box") systems require more scrutiny and more robust safeguards.

Go to Complexity Assessment (Appendix C)
4

Consider the Sector Context

Before assigning a general risk or opportunity score, analyze how the AI tool will change your current practices. An AI system does not exist in a vacuum; its impact is relative to the baseline with which it intersects.

Key Question: A high-stakes context doesn't automatically mean AI increases risk. Rather, the core question is whether AI makes the existing process more or less risky, fair, and effective.

Opportunity Level Performance Improvement Evidence Quality Stakeholder Support Cost-Benefit

High potential value, but also significant risks. Requires the most robust safeguards.

Action: Requires exceptional justification to proceed. If approved, ALL Level 1 AND Level 2 Safeguards must be applied.

Low Risk STANDARD DEPLOY

Action: Proceed with Level 1 Safeguards.

Phase 3

Procurement

If your recommendation is to proceed, you must now navigate the procurement process with the appropriate safeguards and requirements based on your system's classification. The procurement phase establishes the contractual foundation that protects your agency, ensures accountability, and maintains compliance throughout the system's lifecycle.

1

Classification Memo Approval

  • The Classification Memo from Phase 2 must be completed and formally approved by designated authority.
  • The memo establishes the system's risk and opportunity levels, which determine procurement requirements.
2

Budget and Resource Confirmation

Confirm funding is available not just for acquisition, but for:

  • Initial implementation and integration
  • Staff training and change management
  • Ongoing monitoring and oversight
  • Independent evaluations (for Substantial Risk systems)
  • Community engagement processes (for Substantial Risk systems)
3

Procurement Team Assembly

Designate a Procurement Lead with appropriate authority

Include representatives from:

  • Legal/General Counsel
  • End-user departments
  • IT/Technical staff
  • Finance/Budget office

For Substantial Risk systems, add:

  • Community representatives or liaison
  • Independent technical expert (if system is complex)
4

Contract Negotiation and Essential Terms

Contract negotiation must secure all necessary protections and requirements based on your system's classification.

Phase 4

Implementation

With procurement complete and contracts executed, now move to implementation. Implementation is where theoretical safeguards and contractual promises become operational reality. This phase requires careful attention to how the system will actually function in your environment, how people will use it, and how you'll ensure it performs as intended.

Complete the Implementation Memorandum

Before any system goes live, complete the Implementation Memorandum Template. This document serves as your operational blueprint.

Go to Implementation Memo (Appendix G)

Level 1 Implementation Requirements (For All Systems)

These baseline requirements apply to every AI system deployed in criminal justice settings.

Human-Centered Design

  • Design the user interface and workflow to present AI outputs as recommendations, not directives.
  • Show operators the relevant information that informed each recommendation, giving them context to exercise independent judgment.
  • Document every override decision along with the operator's rationale.

Training and Capacity Building

Before anyone operates the AI system, they need training that covers system functionality, limitations, known failure modes, and the common tendency to over-rely on automated suggestions known as automation-bias.

  • Plan for regular refresher training, updates when the system changes, and sessions incorporating findings from ongoing monitoring.
  • Keep detailed records of who has been trained, maintain current training materials, and evaluate training effectiveness.

Creating Transparency and Accountability

The public has a right to know when AI systems affect them. Develop clear public notification in plain language explaining what the system does, how it's used, and who is accountable for decisions involving it.

  • Internally, designate specific officials who are publicly accountable for the system.
  • Document decision-making processes and override rationales.
  • Publish regular reports on system performance, including how often operators override the system and why.

Protecting Privacy and Data Security

Collect and process only what's necessary for the system's legitimate purpose. Regularly review these requirements to prevent unnecessary accumulation.

  • Implement robust technical safeguards per your contract, including regular security assessments, strict access controls, and monitoring for unauthorized access.
  • Establish and follow clear retention policies with automated deletion where appropriate.

Pilot Program

Before deploying at full-scale, test the system in your actual operational environment. Design a pilot with controlled scope and clear success criteria defined before you begin.

  • During the pilot, systematically track your success metrics while remaining alert for unexpected behaviors.
  • Rigorously evaluate results against your success criteria.
  • Make a formal go/no-go decision. If the pilot reveals constitutional violations or unmitigable harms, you cannot proceed.

Ongoing Monitoring and Oversight

  • Continuously monitor system performance, watching for degradation over time.
  • Pay special attention to demographic performance, investigating any differences that emerge.
  • Establish clear procedures for identifying problems, addressing them quickly, and documenting issues and resolutions.

Level 2 Enhanced Requirements (For Substantial Risk Systems)

Systems classified as substantial risk require everything in Level 1 plus these enhanced protections.

Strengthening Rights Protection

Conduct formal legal analysis reviewing due process implications, equal protection concerns, and surveillance or privacy impacts. Ensure individual rights remain fully preserved through clear procedures for people to:

  • Challenge AI-influenced decisions
  • Access meaningful information about how AI was used in their case
  • Present additional or new information

Community Engagement

  • Establish a Community Advisory Committee with representatives from affected communities.
  • Maintain ongoing communication through regular public reports and accessible forums.
  • Document how community concerns are addressed, incorporate feedback into improvements.

Demanding Evidence and Validation

  • Require independent validation by experts not affiliated with your vendor or agency.
  • Conduct formal alternatives analysis examining non-AI approaches and documenting why AI is preferable despite its risks.

Auditing and Enhanced Oversight

  • Provide extended training on system limitations, automation bias resistance.
  • Protect staff through clear whistleblower protections and formal channels for reporting concerns.
  • Implement real-time demographic analysis that continuously monitors performance across groups.
  • Maintain comprehensive documentation including detailed decision logs and complete audit trails.

Phase 5

Ongoing Management and Reassessment

Deployment is not the end of the process. All systems require ongoing monitoring and periodic reassessment to ensure they continue to function as intended without causing harm.

Scheduled Reassessment

Substantial Risk systems must be fully reassessed annually.

Triggered Reassessment

A new, full assessment must be conducted immediately if:

  • System Changes: An update changes the system's core functionality, learning processes, or decision logic.
  • Scope Expansion: The system is applied to new use cases or new populations.
  • Performance Issues: There is evidence of unexpected behaviors, systematic errors, or performance degradation.
  • Rights Concerns: Bias, disparate impacts, or constitutional compliance issues are identified.
  • Environmental Changes: Relevant laws, policies, or the human rights environment shift.

Risk Escalation Protocol

If Reclassified to a Higher Risk Level: You must immediately implement all requirements for the new, higher level. There is no grace period for compliance.

If Reclassified as Prohibited: Cease operations of the system immediately. Follow the protocol in Appendix B.

Open Questions about Institutional Design

This framework provides a structured pathway for responsible AI adoption, but frameworks alone are not sufficient to guarantee good outcomes. We recognize that many criminal justice agencies may lack the internal capacity to fully execute this process without additional support. Key questions remain about what infrastructure is needed to make the full recommendations embedded in this framework practicable. The Task Force flags these institutional design questions as areas for potential future work by this body, successor entities, policymakers, or other stakeholders:

  • Agency capacity: What minimum internal resources and expertise should agencies have before pursuing AI adoption? How can smaller agencies access independent technical and legal expertise?
  • Bundled support: Should professional associations, states, or regions establish centralized review bodies, shared services, pre-approved vendor lists, or pooled technical assistance to reduce the burden on individual agencies?
  • Federal role: What guidance, standards, or grant incentives from federal agencies would support responsible local adoption?
  • Technical assistance: Who should provide implementation support—state agencies, academic institutions, nonprofits, or professional associations—and how should it be structured?
  • Accountability mechanisms: What external oversight from courts, legislatures, or civil society can reinforce these best practices?

Appendix B

Protocol for Prohibited Systems

If your screening has identified a PROHIBITED system, follow this protocol immediately.

1

DO NOT PROCURE OR IMPLEMENT

Halt any procurement process immediately.

2

DOCUMENT THE PROHIBITION

  • Complete the Classification Memo.
  • Clearly state which prohibited indicators applied.
  • Explain why the violations could not be mitigated.
3

EXPLORE ALTERNATIVE SOLUTIONS

  • Revisit the original problem the AI was intended to solve.
  • Explore alternative approaches and non-AI solutions that respect constitutional boundaries.
  • Consult stakeholders to develop preferable alternatives.
4

ORGANIZATIONAL LEARNING

  • Share lessons learned with the organization to prevent future prohibited proposals.
  • Share information about the prohibited application with peers in your sector.

Appendix C

System Complexity and Interpretability Assessment

AI systems vary in their complexity, transparency, and the difficulty of validating their behavior and impact. These characteristics help determine how much scrutiny, testing, and monitoring are needed.

Key Dimensions to Assess

1. Decision Transparency

More Transparent: The system follows clear, documented rules or formulas that can be traced step-by-step. Operators can explain exactly why the system produced a specific output.
Less Transparent: The system uses methods that are opaque or difficult to understand. The exact reasoning path may be unknown or not intuitive to human operators.

2. Predictability and Consistency

More Predictable: Given the same inputs, the system will always produce the same output. Its behavior is stable and well-understood.
Less Predictable: The system may produce novel or unexpected results. It might learn and adapt over time or exhibit behaviors that surprise operators.

3. Validation Difficulty

Easier to Validate: The system's performance can be tested straightforwardly. Errors are easy to identify, and the system's accuracy can be verified through standard quality assurance processes.
Harder to Validate: The system's behavior is difficult to test comprehensively. It may process complex, ambiguous inputs where "correct" outputs are subjective or context-dependent.

4. Adaptability

Static Systems: The system's logic only changes when humans manually reprogram it. Its rules are fixed.
Adaptive Systems: The system may learn from new data and change its behavior over time. This adaptability creates uncertainty about future performance.

Assessment Questions

How to Use This Assessment

Systems with lower complexity (transparent, predictable, easy to validate, static) require standard oversight approaches.

Systems with higher complexity (opaque, ambiguous, hard to validate, processing unstructured data) require enhanced oversight including explainability capabilities, continuous monitoring, and independent technical validation.

Appendix D

Sector Context Guidance

Review this guidance for your specific sector. The key question is marginal change: how does the AI system change risk and opportunity compared to current practices?

Law Enforcement

Risk Assessment

AI may increase risk when it:

  • Enables new scope of surveillance, monitoring, or data aggregation impossible previously
  • Influences stops, searches, or arrests with less review than current practice
  • Aggregates data creating new investigative powers unavailable currently
  • Lacks safeguards present in current processes

AI may maintain or reduce risk when it:

  • Replaces more biased current decision-making with objective criteria
  • Adds accountability and transparency to current practices
  • Operates in administrative functions not affecting current constitutional practices
  • Includes safeguards stronger than current practice
  • Received community support as improvement over current practices

Opportunity Assessment

High opportunity requires demonstrating improvement over current practice:

  • Independent evidence that AI performs better than current methods
  • Reduction in disparate impacts compared to current practice
  • Improved outcomes beyond current baseline
  • Community validation that it addresses real problems with current practices

Courts

Risk Assessment

AI may increase risk when it:

  • Reduces transparency compared to current judicial reasoning
  • Generates recommendations where judges currently exercise fuller discretion
  • Creates information asymmetries beyond those in current practice
  • Is less contestable by defense than current processes
  • Meets lower scientific standards than current requirements

AI may maintain or reduce risk when it:

  • Makes current implicit biases explicit and measurable
  • Provides information improving on current data
  • Improves current adversarial balance
  • Handles purely administrative functions without affecting adjudication processes
  • Reduces unwarranted disparities in current practices through better information

Opportunity Assessment

High opportunity requires demonstrating improvement over current practice:

  • Evidence of better decision quality or efficiency
  • Reduction in unwarranted disparities
  • Enhancement of individualized consideration currently lacking
  • Preserved or improved due process compared to current procedures
  • Validation across demographic groups showing improvement over current baseline outcomes

Corrections

Risk Assessment

AI may increase risk when it:

  • Reduces transparency or contestability compared to current classification processes
  • Restricts programming access beyond current limitations
  • Impacts family connections with fewer exceptions than current policy
  • Reduces rehabilitation focus below current practice standards

AI may maintain or reduce risk when it:

  • Makes current classifications more auditable
  • Identifies service needs better than current informal processes
  • Improves program matching beyond current assignments
  • Operates in administrative functions not affecting current individual experiences
  • Enhances family connections beyond current restrictive practices
  • Includes stronger protections than current practice for vulnerable populations

Opportunity Assessment

High opportunity requires demonstrating improvement over current practice:

  • Evidence of better reentry outcomes than current baseline recidivism
  • Improved program matching compared to current assignment processes
  • Enhanced identification of service needs currently missed
  • Demonstrated benefits for incarcerated individuals beyond current programming
  • Significant, demonstrable efficiency gains

Community Organizations

Risk Assessment

AI may increase risk when it:

  • Introduces monitoring beyond current trust-based relationships
  • Creates service access barriers not present in current practice
  • Shares data with justice agencies beyond current sharing
  • Reduces human interaction
  • Makes participation less voluntary than current processes
  • Serves vulnerable populations with fewer protections than current practice
  • Damages trust or advocacy reputation more than current practices
  • Represents funder-driven mission drift from current community-driven approach

AI may maintain or reduce risk when it:

  • Improves service matching beyond current processes
  • Identifies needs currently missed in programs
  • Reduces bias in current service decisions
  • Enhances rather than replaces current relationships
  • Maintains or exceeds current privacy protections
  • Increases transparency beyond current processes
  • Increases individual agency and choice compared to current options
  • Better supports both service and advocacy missions than current approach

Opportunity Assessment

High opportunity requires demonstrating improvement over current practice:

  • Evidence of better individual outcomes than current service delivery baseline
  • Increased access to services compared to current capacity
  • Validation from participants that it addresses real gaps in current practice
  • Confirmation it enhances rather than replaces current relationship-building
  • Evidence it strengthens rather than compromises current advocacy effectiveness
  • Demonstration it responds to community-identified needs not adequately met by current practice

How to Apply This Guidance

  1. Identify your current practice baseline: What happens now without AI? How are decisions currently made?
  2. Assess marginal change: How specifically will AI change current practice? What risks does it add, maintain, or reduce?
  3. Document your reasoning in the Classification Memo explaining which sector factors influenced your final classification.

General Project Management Practices

This AI-specific framework builds upon, and does not replace, standard project management and government procurement practices. Key practices that form the foundation for this framework include:

  • Pre-Procurement Planning: Conducting a needs assessment, defining clear objectives, engaging stakeholders, and creating a budget and timeline.
  • Standard Procurement Process: Market research, RFI/RFP development, vendor evaluation against defined criteria, and contract negotiation.
  • Implementation Best Practices: Using a phased rollout approach, establishing user training programs, and managing change within the organization.
  • General Oversight and Monitoring: Regular performance reviews, budget tracking, and stakeholder communication.
